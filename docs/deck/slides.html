The rule that governs every button you ship

WCAG 2.2, SC 2.5.8, Level AA.

Every accessibility checker on the market measures your controls against 24 by 24 CSS pixels and prints a green tick. axe-core, Lighthouse, whatever your company pays for.

Its published basis is Parhi, Karlson & Bederson, MobileHCI 2006: a one-handed thumb study of able-bodied participants on small touchscreens.

axe-core's own rule page names "people who have mobility impairments such as hand tremors" as the beneficiaries, and then applies 24 px to everyone.

24 px = 6.35 mm of glass01

Why a square minimum cannot see it

The web is built from wide, short buttons.

A 141 by 30 control passes, because both sides clear 24. The short side is what a hand actually has to hold, and a single number for "target size" hides which one it is.

On the Social Security Administration's homepage: 108 controls, zero fail the standard, 95 pass it and still cannot be held.

ssa.gov, scanned live, 5.1 seconds05

What we shipped

A finding, and the instrument that produced it.

Web

Paste any URL. Real Chromium loads it, waits for fonts, measures every control as rendered, and replays the recording against each rectangle. About three seconds.

Everything on the page is recomputed in your browser from the committed recordings. Nothing is precomputed.

Android

Measures your hand from the phone's accelerometer, works out the button size you need, then checks that prediction against your own taps.

On a touchscreen a millimetre of finger is a millimetre off target, with no counts-per-inch to sweep. Calibrate against a bank card and the millimetres are real.

What we got wrong, and how we know

An adversarial review nearly killed this.

A Hann window never removeda true 2.000 mm read as 0.778 mm at the record's edge
Then: we fixed a confound the data lacksthe filter assumed gravity; PADS ships none, and reported 73° of tilt on a still wrist
A robots.txt matchersilently excluded 18 of 40 sites, all wrongly
DNS rebinding in the scannertwo lookups, and an attacker answers them differently

The second was found in the round that reviewed the fix for the first. A synthetic test is made out of your assumption, so it can never tell you the assumption is false.

Who it is for

About 11 million hands in the US alone.

Roughly 10 million people live with essential tremor and 1 million with Parkinson's. Every one of them is currently told by an automated green check that your 24-pixel button is fine.

The fix is not a new standard. It is a number that was never checked, and a tool that checks it in three seconds against a page you already shipped.
